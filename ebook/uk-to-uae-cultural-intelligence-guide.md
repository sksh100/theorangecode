# Chapter 1 – Your Move & Your Mindset

### 1.1 Why the UAE attracts British professionals

From the outside, the UAE can look like a mix of skyscrapers, sunshine and salary packages. For many British professionals, the attraction is more layered.

Common reasons British people choose the UAE include:

- Professional growth, with access to international schools, hospitals, companies and projects  
- Tax advantages, which can support savings, mortgages or school fees back home  
- A sense of adventure, living in a region that is dynamic and globally connected  
- A family friendly environment, with modern infrastructure and a strong sense of safety  
- Strategic location, with relatively easy travel to Europe, Asia and Africa

For many, the UAE offers a fresh start. You can renew your professional identity, build a more international network and expose yourself and your family to new languages and perspectives.

At the same time, this move means stepping into a culture that is proud, rooted and shaped by values that may differ from what you are used to in the UK. Understanding and honouring this is essential.

### 1.2 Biggest culture shocks UK expats report

British expats often describe similar “surprise moments” in their first months. Here are some you may recognise:

- **Different sense of time**  
  Meetings that start later than expected, or run in a different order from the agenda. Situations where “tomorrow” can mean “soon, but not fixed”.

- **Communication style**  
  People who are warm and polite, but rarely say a direct “no”. Feedback that is given indirectly or privately rather than in open meetings.

- **Hierarchy and decision making**  
  Stronger emphasis on titles, seniority and age. Decisions that are finalised by people who are not in the room, after relationship building has taken place.

- **Public etiquette**  
  Clear expectations around dress, public behaviour and respect for religious practices. A legal environment that is strict about public conduct and reputation.

- **Social separation**  
  The feeling of expat “bubbles”, alongside an Emirati society that is close knit and values privacy.

These are not problems to be solved. They are features of a particular cultural landscape. Your task is to learn to navigate them with curiosity rather than judgement.

### 1.3 Myths UK people have about the UAE

Many first impressions of the UAE in the UK are shaped by headlines and second hand stories. Before you arrive, it is useful to surface common myths and replace them with more nuanced views.

**Myth 1: “The UAE is the same everywhere”**  
Reality: Each emirate has its own history, leadership, priorities and local culture. Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah and the other emirates share a national identity, but daily life and workplace culture can feel different. Even within one city, organisations vary widely.

**Myth 2: “The UAE is only luxury malls and brunches”**  
Reality: The modern skyline is real, but so are the deeper cultural layers. Hospitality tents, traditional neighbourhoods, mosques, family gatherings and local customs continue to shape people’s lives. Focusing only on the glitter gives you a narrow view.

**Myth 3: “It will feel like being on holiday all the time”**  
Reality: Once you are working full time, dealing with school runs or night shifts, it becomes real life. You will manage budgets, deadlines, traffic and difficult days, just in a different place and climate. Treating the UAE as a serious, long term home is more respectful and more sustainable.

**Myth 4: “Everyone thinks the same way here”**  
Reality: The UAE is one of the most multicultural countries in the world. You will work with Emiratis, Arabs from the wider region, South Asians, Africans, Europeans, North Americans and others. There is no single “UAE mindset”. You need to understand both local culture and multicultural dynamics.

### 1.4 What Cultural Intelligence (CQ) means

Cultural intelligence, often shortened to CQ, is not simply “being nice to people from other countries”. It is your capacity to read cultural cues, adapt your behaviour thoughtfully and still remain authentic.

You can think of CQ in four parts:

- **Drive**  
  Your motivation to engage with cultural differences rather than avoid them. Curiosity, openness and willingness to learn.

- **Knowledge**  
  Your understanding of how cultures differ in values, communication, hierarchy and decision making. In this guide you will learn patterns that often shape behaviour in the UAE.

- **Strategy**  
  Your habit of pausing, observing and planning your response, instead of reacting automatically with your usual UK patterns.

- **Action**  
  Your ability to adjust how you speak, listen, give feedback and build relationships, in ways that are respectful of the local context.

High CQ does not mean you become someone else. It means you extend your range, in a considered way. You will still be British. You will simply be a British person who knows how to work well in the UAE.

### 1.5 How to get the most from this guide

To turn this guide into real change, treat it as a workbook as well as a book.

- Read actively, not passively. Underline phrases that challenge you. Note examples that feel uncomfortable or unfamiliar.  
- Link ideas to real people. As you read about hierarchy or communication, think of colleagues, parents, patients or clients you already know or expect to meet.  
- Use the scenarios. When you encounter the practical scenarios in later chapters, pause and ask yourself “What would I normally do?” before reading the culturally aligned response.  
- Repeat, do not rush. You do not need to memorise everything at once. Reread key sections after your first week, first month and first 90 days.  
- Combine with conversation. Use this guide as a starting point for respectful questions with colleagues, HR or local friends.

### 1.6 Reflection: excitement vs concerns

Before you move, take a piece of paper and divide it into two columns.

At the top of the left column, write: “What excites me about moving to the UAE”.  
At the top of the right column, write: “What I am concerned about”.

In the left column, list as many items as you can. Examples might include:

- Building my savings  
- Teaching in a diverse classroom  
- Working in a modern hospital  
- Living in the sun  
- Travelling regionally

In the right column, list worries such as:

- Saying something inappropriate without realising  
- Navigating gender norms  
- Coping with the heat  
- Children settling at school  
- Managing distance from family in the UK

Once you have your list, circle the three items in each column that feel most important. For each of these six points, note which chapter of this guide might help you address it. For example, worries about communication link to Chapters 3 and 5, and concerns about life outside work link to Chapter 8.

Keep this page. After three months in the UAE, revisit it and write a short note next to each item: “as expected”, “easier than expected”, or “different from what I imagined”. This will show you how your mindset has evolved.

# Chapter 2 – UAE Cultural Foundations

### 2.1 Short history of the UAE

The United Arab Emirates is a relatively young country in its modern form. The federation was established in 1971, bringing together seven emirates along the Arabian Gulf coastline. Before unification, the area was known for its pearling, fishing and trading communities, as well as for its tribal structures and links to the wider region.

Rapid development over the last few decades has transformed the physical landscape. Cities like Abu Dhabi and Dubai have grown into global hubs for business, tourism and aviation. Yet beneath the skyscrapers, there is a strong sense of continuity. Tribal identities, family ties, hospitality and religious practice remain central.

Understanding the UAE as both modern and deeply rooted will help you avoid the mistake of seeing it as either “old fashioned” or “only shiny and new”. It is both, in different ways.

### 2.2 Core cultural values

While every individual is unique, certain values are widely recognised as important in Emirati society.

**Respect**  
Showing respect for elders, leaders, religious figures, teachers and guests is central. Respect is communicated through language, body posture, tone of voice and the way you handle disagreement. Speaking politely, avoiding public criticism and acknowledging someone’s status are all part of this.

**Dignity**  
Protecting one’s own dignity and that of others is crucial. People take care not to embarrass or shame others in public. This influences feedback, conflict management and the handling of mistakes. An approach that feels “refreshingly honest” in a British context can feel harsh or humiliating in a UAE workplace.

**Hospitality**  
Welcoming guests warmly has deep cultural roots. Serving coffee, offering dates, inviting people to sit and talk, and making time for relationship building are expressions of generosity and honour. Accepting hospitality with gratitude is important.

**Family**  
Family ties are strong. Extended family relationships shape decision making, support systems and social life. Weekends and holidays often revolve around family gatherings. Even in business, decisions can be influenced by family considerations.

**Faith**  
Islam shapes many aspects of daily life, from prayer times to holidays. You will hear the call to prayer, see people take breaks for worship, and notice references to God in everyday language. Respectful awareness of this is expected, even if you are not Muslim.

These values are not abstract. They influence how people greet each other, what is discussed in public, how conflicts are managed and what is considered appropriate.

### 2.3 Islam as a cultural framework

Islam is not only a personal belief system. In the UAE it also provides a shared reference point for ethics, community life and public behaviour.

A few practical points:

- Prayer times occur five times a day. You may see colleagues leave for a few minutes to pray in a designated room. Meetings and events may be scheduled around key prayer times on Fridays.  
- The holy month of Ramadan is especially significant. Muslims fast from dawn to sunset and focus on reflection, charity and family. Working hours, school timetables and social routines may shift.  
- Expressions such as “Inshallah” (if God wills) or “Alhamdulillah” (praise be to God) are common in conversation. They reflect humility and recognition that outcomes are in God’s hands.

You are not expected to participate in religious practices, but you are expected to respect them. Avoid jokes, casual comments or comparisons that could be interpreted as dismissive.

### 2.4 Tribal and family structures

Historically, life in the region was organised around tribes and extended families. These structures fostered resilience in a challenging environment. Trust was built over generations, and loyalty to family and tribe was essential.

Though modern institutions now play a larger role, a sense of belonging to a wider family and tribal network remains significant. This can influence:

- Who is trusted with sensitive information  
- How quickly decisions are made  
- Who is involved in high level discussions  
- How people view responsibility to relatives

As a British professional, you are unlikely to see all the details of these dynamics, and it is not your place to comment on them. What you can do is notice that relationships and reputation travel through networks that may not be visible to you. This is one reason why public behaviour and social media presence matter.

### 2.5 Emirati society and expat society

The UAE has a high proportion of expatriate residents from many countries. However, it is important to remember that this is an Emirati country, with its own citizens, history and priorities.

You may notice:

- Emirati neighbourhoods and gathering places that are largely separate from some expat areas  
- Emiratis working in leadership, government roles and key positions across sectors  
- Expat communities organised around nationality, language or profession

As a visitor or resident, it is respectful to learn basic facts about the UAE, its leaders, its flag, national anthem and key historical moments, such as National Day. Taking an interest communicates respect.

At the same time, recognise that you are entering an environment where many cultures meet. There is no single “expat culture”. You will collaborate with people whose own cultural assumptions may be quite different from yours, as well as from Emirati norms.

### 2.6 How this compares to typical UK values

It can help to place UK and UAE tendencies side by side, not to judge them, but to understand them.

Simplifying for illustration:

- The UK often values individual autonomy, straightforwardness and a degree of informality. People may show respect by being relaxed and “on the same level”.  
- The UAE often places stronger emphasis on visible respect, formality in certain settings, and the importance of family and faith in public life.

Where a British person might think “We are all equal, so I speak openly to my manager”, a colleague in the UAE may think “Out of respect, I will be more cautious in how I speak to a senior”.

Where a British team might prefer quick, transparent decisions, a UAE based team might give more time for consultation and relationship building before a conclusion is reached.

# Chapter 3 – Communication Differences: UK vs UAE

### 3.1 High context and low context communication

In some cultures, most of the message is contained in the words themselves. In others, a lot of meaning is carried by tone, body language, context and shared understanding.

British communication, especially in professional settings, often aims for clarity and explicitness, though it retains its own indirect habits. In many settings in the UAE, and particularly in Arabic speaking contexts, communication can be more high context. This means:

- More is implied than said directly  
- People pay close attention to how something is said, not only to the words  
- A polite phrase may carry a clear message once you know how to read it

As a British professional, it is easy to focus on the literal content and miss what is being conveyed between the lines. This chapter will help you tune your ear.

### 3.2 British directness and Emirati diplomacy

British people often see themselves as polite, but also appreciate “getting to the point”. In some cases, this can feel quite direct to others.

In parts of the UAE, there can be a stronger preference for diplomacy and the protection of dignity. This may mean:

- Avoiding blunt refusals  
- Wrapping criticism in positive language  
- Choosing a private setting for sensitive conversations

An example:

- UK instinct: “This report is not good enough. The data is wrong and the deadline was missed. We need to talk about your performance.”  
- UAE aligned approach: “Thank you for your efforts on the report. There are a few areas we need to strengthen, especially the data and timing. Let us sit together to see how we can improve it for next time.”

The content is similar, but the second version protects the person’s dignity in a way that is more consistent with local expectations.

### 3.3 Reading between the lines

Because maintaining good relationships and dignity is important, people may use phrases that sound neutral or positive, but carry a clear meaning in context. For example:

- “Let us revisit this later” may mean “Not now, and maybe not at all”.  
- “We will see” may gently signal doubt, without a firm commitment.  
- “It might be challenging” can be a polite way of saying “very unlikely” or “not advisable”.

As you work with colleagues, pay attention to patterns. Notice how often something actually happens after being phrased in a particular way. Over time, you will learn to interpret these cues more accurately.

### 3.4 Non verbal communication

Body language, eye contact and physical space also carry meaning.

A few general points:

- Standing up to greet someone, offering a warm handshake when appropriate, and using a calm tone of voice all communicate respect.  
- Some colleagues may prefer less intense direct eye contact, especially in cross gender interactions in certain settings.  
- Same gender colleagues who know each other well may stand closer than you are used to in the UK.  
- Public displays of frustration, such as raising your voice or showing obvious annoyance, can damage your reputation more quickly than you might expect.

If in doubt, aim for calm, steady, respectful presence.

### 3.5 Emotional expression in UAE workplaces

In many UAE workplaces, you may see a wider range of emotional expression than in some British offices. People may:

- Speak with more warmth and enthusiasm  
- Show care for colleagues’ personal lives more openly  
- Take time for informal conversation before moving to the task

At the same time, there can be strong self control when it comes to public disagreement or embarrassment. Showing anger in front of others can be seen as a lack of self discipline.

Your challenge is to balance British reserve with genuine warmth, and to channel frustration into private, solution focused conversations.

### 3.6 Email, WhatsApp and phone etiquette

Digital communication is critical in the UAE, especially in busy, fast moving environments.

Some practical guidelines:

- **Email**  
  Use courteous openings and closings. Titles and names matter. Address people as “Dear Dr Ahmed”, “Dear Mr Al Mansoori” or “Dear Ms Khan” unless invited to use first names. Keep criticism out of group emails.  
- **WhatsApp**  
  Many teams use WhatsApp groups for quick coordination. Be aware of working hours, and follow your organisation’s guidance on out of hours messages. Avoid sending sensitive feedback or criticism in group chats.  
- **Phone calls**  
  In some situations, a brief phone call is considered more efficient and more respectful than lengthy back and forth messages. If you have to cancel or change a meeting at short notice, a call can be better than an email.

When in doubt, ask colleagues what is considered normal in your setting.

### 3.7 Useful scripts: polite “no”, soft disagreement, raising concerns

Having a few phrases ready can make it easier to adapt your style.

**Politely saying “no” or pushing back**

Instead of: “No, I cannot do that.”  
Try:

- “I would like to help, but I am concerned about the timing. Could we look at an alternative date?”  
- “At the moment, my capacity is limited. Is it possible to prioritise A and B, and postpone C?”

**Softening disagreement**

Instead of: “You are wrong, that will not work.”  
Try:

- “I see your point, and I have a slightly different perspective. May I share it?”  
- “Thank you for the suggestion. I wonder if there is another option that could also support our goals.”

**Raising concerns**

Instead of: “This is unacceptable.”  
Try:

- “I appreciate the effort that has gone into this. I do have a few concerns that I would like to discuss, especially around X and Y. Could we find a good time for a private conversation?”

# Chapter 4 – Trust, Relationships & Hierarchy at Work

### 4.1 Why trust comes before tasks

In many British workplaces, it is acceptable to dive straight into tasks and deliverables, then build relationships over time. In parts of the UAE, especially in relationship driven sectors, trust often comes first.

This means that colleagues and partners may:

- Want to get to know you as a person before agreeing to major decisions  
- Take time for conversation before moving to the agenda in meetings  
- Place significant weight on personal recommendations and introductions

If you insist on “getting straight to the point” without investing in connection, you may be seen as efficient but cold. Building trust is not a distraction from work; it is part of the work.

### 4.2 Relationship building rituals

Small rituals matter. You may encounter:

- Being invited for Arabic coffee, dates or tea before a meeting  
- Polite questions about your family, your experience in the UAE or your impressions of the country so far  
- Offers of assistance such as “Let us know if you need anything” which are sincere

Engage with these moments. Simple behaviours like accepting coffee with thanks, showing genuine interest in colleagues’ stories and remembering names will deepen trust.

### 4.3 Status, age, titles and respect

Titles and forms of address are important signals of respect. In many settings:

- Senior leaders are addressed by titles such as “Doctor”, “Engineer”, “Professor” or “Sheikh”, where appropriate  
- Older colleagues are given verbal and non verbal signs of respect, such as allowing them to enter a room first  
- Interrupting or contradicting someone significantly more senior in front of others can be seen as disrespectful

This does not mean that juniors have no voice. It means that how and where you express disagreement matters. Private conversations, careful wording and acknowledgement of someone’s status are key.

### 4.4 UK flat culture and UAE hierarchy

Many British workplaces present themselves as relatively flat. People call each other by first name, and managers might say “My door is always open”. While power structures still exist, they are often less formally visible.

In the UAE, hierarchy can be more clearly expressed, even in organisations that value collaboration. Roles, ranks and reporting lines matter. At the same time, many leaders are approachable and open to input when it is offered respectfully.

As a British professional, you may need to:

- Be more deliberate in how you prepare for meetings with senior figures  
- Coordinate with your line manager before making direct approaches to very senior people  
- Think more carefully about the order in which you raise points in group settings

### 4.5 How decisions are made

In UK environments, decisions are often made in the meeting itself. The discussion is visible and the conclusion is recorded.

In some UAE settings, what appears to be a decision making meeting is more of a consultation step. People share views, positions are sensed, and the final decision may be made later by senior leaders, sometimes in a smaller setting.

This can feel unclear if you are used to more transparent processes. Instead of pushing for immediate closure, you might:

- Ask “What are the next steps, and who will confirm the final decision?”  
- Follow up respectfully if you have not heard back by the suggested time  
- Understand that relationship work behind the scenes is part of the process

### 4.6 Building credibility as a British professional

Credibility in the UAE comes from more than your CV. It is built through repeated interactions where people see that you:

- Deliver on your promises  
- Communicate with respect and discretion  
- Show loyalty to your team and organisation  
- Demonstrate care for the local context and culture

A few early credibility builders:

- Learn how to pronounce colleagues’ names correctly  
- Show that you have done basic research on the UAE and the emirate you are in  
- Ask thoughtful questions about how things are done locally, rather than assuming UK methods are always best

### Scenario 1 – The impatient meeting

You are a British project manager in Dubai. You have a one hour meeting with a senior client representative. The first twenty minutes are spent on general conversation about family, travel and impressions of the city. You are worried about time and keen to go through your slides.

UK instinct: Move quickly to “Thank you, shall we start the presentation?” and drive through the content to “stay on track”.

UAE aligned approach: You recognise that these first twenty minutes are the relationship building phase. You engage fully, while gently signalling that you are ready to share your ideas. For example, “It is lovely to hear about your family. Thank you again for your time today. When you are ready, I would be happy to walk you through the proposal.”

### Scenario 2 – Public disagreement

You are a British teacher in Abu Dhabi. In a staff meeting, a senior colleague presents a new policy. You can see several practical issues. In the UK you might challenge the idea openly to show engagement.

UK instinct: “I do not think this will work as it is. There are too many problems.”

UAE aligned approach: You listen carefully, take notes and ask clarifying questions that help the group think. Afterwards, you approach the senior colleague privately and say, “Thank you for the presentation. I had a few concerns about implementation, especially in Year 9. May I share some thoughts and possible solutions with you?”

### Scenario 3 – Building trust with a senior doctor

You are a British nurse working with a senior Emirati doctor. In your first week, you focus on doing your tasks well and staying out of the way.

UK instinct: Assume that your competence will be noticed eventually and remain quiet.

UAE aligned approach: You continue to perform clinically, but you also make a point of greeting the doctor respectfully, asking brief, relevant questions and showing interest in their guidance. Over time, you might say, “I appreciate how you handle families when they are upset. If you have any feedback on how I communicate, I would be grateful to learn.”

# Chapter 5 – Meetings, Time & Feedback

### 5.1 Time perception in the UAE

Time is experienced differently in different cultures. In some places, clocks and schedules are central. In others, relationships and events are given priority, and the clock is more flexible.

In the UAE, you will see both:

- Highly punctual environments, for example in aviation, healthcare and some corporate settings  
- More flexible timing in contexts where relationship building and external factors play a stronger role

As a British professional, you should continue to be punctual, especially in your own commitments. At the same time, you will need to develop tolerance for delays, last minute changes and rescheduling. Taking these personally will increase stress and can damage relationships.

### 5.2 What “Inshallah” can actually mean

“Inshallah” literally means “if God wills”. It reflects recognition that outcomes depend on God, not only on our plans.

In practice, “Inshallah” can be used in several ways, depending on tone, context and relationship:

- Genuine intention: “We will do everything we can to make this happen, and the rest is in God’s hands.”  
- Hopeful possibility: “We would like this to happen, but we cannot guarantee it.”  
- Softening uncertainty: “We are not sure yet, and we will see.”

As you listen, pay attention to how firmly other details are given. If someone says “Inshallah, we will meet on Tuesday at 10, in the meeting room on the second floor” and sends a calendar invite, they probably mean it seriously. If the time is vague and there is no follow up, you may need a gentle reminder closer to the date.

### 5.3 How meetings run in UAE workplaces

Meetings may differ from what you are used to.

You may notice:

- A longer opening phase dedicated to greetings and informal conversation  
- People entering or leaving due to overlapping commitments  
- Decisions being signalled but not always finalised verbally in the room

Practical tips:

- Arrive on time and be prepared, even if others join a little later  
- Bring printed or digital copies of key documents  
- If you are leading the meeting, allow space for relationship building at the start  
- Summarise key points at the end and agree on next steps, including who will confirm decisions

### 5.4 Giving and receiving feedback without damage

Feedback is essential in any workplace, but its style varies significantly by culture.

In the UK, relatively direct feedback, including in group settings, may be considered evidence of professionalism and clarity. In many UAE contexts, public criticism can feel harsh and humiliating.

Better approaches include:

- Giving constructive feedback in private, with a calm tone  
- Framing feedback around improvement and shared goals  
- Balancing critique with acknowledgement of effort and strengths

For example:

Instead of: “You are always late with reports, it is unacceptable.”  
Try: “I value the detail you put into your reports. One area we need to improve is timing, as deadlines are critical for the wider team. Let us look together at how we can make this easier to manage.”

When receiving feedback yourself, remember that it may come in a more indirect form. Listen carefully for comments such as “perhaps we can strengthen this part” or “next time we might try a different approach”. These may be signalling that change is genuinely needed.

### 5.5 Handling conflict diplomatically

Disagreements are inevitable. In the UAE, as in many places, how you handle them can have long term consequences.

Guidelines:

- Avoid raising your voice or showing anger in front of others  
- Avoid emails written while emotional; if you must write, pause and re read later before sending  
- Seek private, face to face conversation where possible  
- Acknowledge the other person’s perspective before sharing your own  
- Suggest possible solutions and be open to theirs

### 5.6 Mixed multicultural teams

In the UAE, you are likely to work in teams that include Emiratis and people from many other countries. Each brings their own cultural assumptions about leadership, time, feedback and risk.

This can lead to misunderstandings such as:

- One group expecting very direct instructions, another preferring more autonomy  
- Some colleagues seeing silence as agreement, while others see it as careful consideration  
- Different comfort levels with raising concerns in front of the group

As a British professional, you can help by:

- Making expectations explicit in a respectful way  
- Checking understanding by asking people to summarise agreed actions  
- Encouraging contributions from quieter voices, while avoiding putting individuals on the spot

### 5.7 Case studies: nurse, teacher, sales professional

**Case study 1 – Nurse**

Amelia, a British nurse, joins a busy ward in Abu Dhabi. In the UK she is used to short, focused handovers. Here, she notices that handovers include more storytelling, and that families often ask to speak to the doctor rather than the nurse.

At first, she feels that time is being wasted. Over time, she realises that these stories carry important information about family expectations and patient concerns. She learns to listen for underlying worries and to keep a calm, reassuring tone, even when delivering difficult news. She gives feedback to junior staff privately, with care for their dignity.

**Case study 2 – Teacher**

James, a British teacher, starts work in a Dubai school. In the first parent meeting, a father arrives late, and the conversation begins with warm greetings and discussion of family rather than grades.

James adjusts his British instinct to move straight to “results”. He allows time for rapport, acknowledges the family’s hopes for their child and then shares his observations. When raising concerns about behaviour, he chooses his words carefully to avoid making the child feel shamed, focusing on future improvements rather than past failings.

**Case study 3 – Sales professional**

Hannah, a British sales manager, is used to quick introductory calls followed by online proposals. In the UAE, she finds that corporate clients expect more in person meetings, sometimes over coffee, before discussing numbers.

She invests time in these meetings, listens more than she speaks and asks respectful questions about the client’s long term goals. She learns that decisions may depend on relationships at senior levels. Instead of pushing aggressively for a signature, she checks in diplomatically and positions herself as a trusted partner.

# Chapter 6 – Dress Code, Etiquette & Gender Norms

### 6.1 Public etiquette

Public behaviour in the UAE is shaped by respect for local customs and laws.

Key points:

- Public displays of affection should be modest. Holding hands between married couples is generally accepted, but more intimate behaviour is not appropriate in public places.  
- Use respectful language in public. Swearing, insulting gestures and loud arguments can have legal consequences.  
- Be mindful of your surroundings when speaking critically about any country, culture, religion or leader.  
- Photographing people, especially families, without their consent is considered disrespectful.

### 6.2 Dress code for men and women

Dress expectations vary by context, but modesty is a consistent theme.

General guidelines:

- **Workplace**  
  Men: long trousers, shirts with sleeves, closed shoes.  
  Women: skirts or trousers that reach at least to the knee, tops with sleeves, no low necklines.  
- **Public spaces**  
  Shopping malls, public offices and family areas expect similar standards. Beachwear is appropriate only at pools and beaches, not in malls or restaurants.

When in doubt, observe how local colleagues dress and choose a slightly more conservative option. Covering shoulders and knees in most public and professional contexts is a safe baseline.

### 6.3 Male female interactions

Gender norms in the UAE reflect respect for privacy and modesty, rooted in culture and faith.

Practical points:

- Handshakes between men and women are common in some settings and less so in others. If you are unsure, wait for the other person to initiate. If they do not extend a hand, a polite verbal greeting with a nod and smile is appropriate.  
- Avoid unnecessary physical contact, such as touching someone’s arm to emphasise a point.  
- In mixed meetings, ensure that seating and interaction feel comfortable for everyone, without making assumptions.

If you are ever uncertain, discreetly ask HR or a trusted colleague for guidance.

### 6.4 Social media and reputation

Your online presence travels with you. In a country where reputation and dignity are highly valued, social media content can have real consequences.

Consider:

- Avoid posting negative comments about your employer, colleagues, local customs or the country.  
- Think carefully before sharing photos of others, especially if they are from the local community.  
- Remember that even private groups can become public. Screenshots move quickly.

A helpful question is: “Would I be comfortable if my manager, my colleagues and a UAE official saw this post?”

### 6.5 Alcohol, nightlife and hosting guests

Licensed venues serve alcohol in the UAE, and many expatriates enjoy nightlife. At the same time, alcohol is regulated, and public drunkenness is not tolerated.

Guidelines:

- Drink responsibly and stay in control  
- Avoid driving after drinking; arrange safe transport in advance  
- Keep noise levels respectful when returning home late at night, especially in residential areas

When hosting guests in your home:

- Be mindful of neighbours and shared spaces  
- Be considerate in your use of communal facilities

### 6.6 Ramadan etiquette

During Ramadan:

- Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. As a non Muslim, you are not required to fast, but you should avoid eating, drinking or smoking in public during fasting hours.  
- Your organisation may adjust working hours.  
- Evenings are often dedicated to family and communal meals, known as iftar. Traffic patterns can change around sunset.

Showing consideration, for example by avoiding scheduling demanding sessions just before sunset, will be appreciated.

>  
> Some actions may seem minor from a UK perspective, but can feel significant in the UAE:  
> – Making jokes about religion, even lightly.  
> – Posting criticism of local customs or leadership on social media.  
> – Ignoring dress codes in public settings.  
> – Showing visible frustration with service staff.  
> – Using very direct or sarcastic language in group emails.  
>  
> Everyone makes mistakes. The key is to learn quickly, apologise sincerely if needed and avoid repeating them.

# Chapter 7 – Sector Snapshots (Profession Specific Guidance)

### 7.1 Healthcare (nurses, doctors, families)

**Scenario**

You are a British nurse caring for a patient whose extended family often visit. There are many relatives in the room, asking questions and offering opinions. In the UK you might feel this is disruptive.

UK instinct: Ask some family members to leave, in order to keep only one or two present, and insist on structured visiting.

UAE aligned response: You recognise that family involvement is central to the patient’s wellbeing. You explain hospital policy calmly and respectfully, and where possible, work with the family as partners in care. For example, you might say, “I know your family cares deeply. For the patient’s comfort we need to limit visitors at certain times. Perhaps two of you can stay now, and the others can return later.”

Key points:

- Address family members respectfully  
- Avoid criticising family dynamics  
- Discuss sensitive information privately and discreetly

### 7.2 Education (British teachers)

**Scenario**

You are a British teacher in a UAE school. A parent is upset about grades. In the UK you might respond directly, defending your marking.

UK instinct: “The grades are accurate, and this is the level your child is currently achieving.”

UAE aligned response: You acknowledge the parent’s hopes and show you are on the same side. “I can see how much you care about your child’s progress. Let us look together at where they are doing well and where we can support them further. We can create a plan that helps them move closer to what you are hoping for.”

Key points:

- Emphasise partnership with parents  
- Protect the child’s dignity  
- Avoid public criticism in front of other parents or pupils

### 7.3 Corporate, sales and business development

**Scenario**

You are a British sales manager pitching to a company in Dubai. You prepare a detailed slide deck and want to go through all thirty slides.

UK instinct: Focus on getting through the full presentation, leaving questions for the end.

UAE aligned response: You keep the deck ready, but you prioritise conversation. You start with a concise overview, then invite questions and adapt. You pay attention to what matters most to the client and are prepared to set aside parts of the deck if the discussion goes elsewhere.

Key points:

- Build relationships with multiple stakeholders  
- Be patient with longer decision timelines  
- Show reliability and discretion over time

### 7.4 Government and semi government projects

Work with government or semi government entities often involves:

- Formal processes and protocols  
- Particular attention to hierarchy and titles  
- Strong expectations of confidentiality and alignment with national priorities

As a British professional:

- Learn the correct titles and forms of address  
- Prepare thoroughly for meetings and keep written records of agreed actions  
- Show that you understand and respect the strategic goals of the organisation and the country

### 7.5 Entrepreneurs and consultants

If you are an entrepreneur or consultant:

- Invest time in building a trusted network, not just chasing single contracts  
- Respect local licensing and regulatory requirements  
- Be clear about your value, but modest in self promotion. Let results and recommendations speak for you

Each sector has its own nuances. When you arrive, look for mentors who understand both the local context and your profession. Combine their guidance with the frameworks in this guide.

# Chapter 8 – Life Outside Work: Settling in the UAE

### 8.1 Housing and neighbourhood culture

Housing options range from apartments in high rise buildings to villas in quieter areas. When choosing where to live:

- Consider commute times, especially in heat and traffic  
- Look at access to schools, parks and community facilities  
- Notice the mix of residents, for example families, singles, different nationalities

Neighbour interactions may be more reserved than in some UK communities, especially in large apartment blocks, but courtesy matters. Simple habits such as greeting neighbours, keeping communal areas tidy and respecting quiet hours build a positive environment.

### 8.2 Expat bubbles and local integration

It is easy to live in an expatriate bubble, surrounded mostly by people from similar backgrounds. This can be comfortable, but limits your understanding of the UAE.

To integrate more:

- Attend cultural events, exhibitions and public lectures  
- Explore museums and heritage sites  
- Learn a few phrases of Arabic. Even simple greetings show respect  
- Be curious about local customs and ask questions respectfully

At the same time, accept that there are spaces and gatherings that are primarily for Emirati families and communities. Integration is about respect and relationship, not about entering every space.

### 8.3 Children, schools and transition

If you are moving with children:

- Talk to them early about the move, at a level appropriate to their age  
- Emphasise the adventure and new friends while acknowledging their feelings about leaving the UK  
- Choose schools carefully, looking at both academic standards and pastoral care

Once in the UAE:

- Keep routines where possible, such as regular bedtimes and family meals  
- Help children notice positive aspects of their new environment, such as new activities or diversity  
- Stay attentive to signs of stress or withdrawal and seek support if needed

### 8.4 Safety and law awareness

The UAE is often perceived as a safe country. Many residents appreciate low levels of street crime and well maintained public spaces.

However, safety also depends on understanding and respecting local laws. This includes:

- Laws related to public behaviour and decency  
- Traffic regulations, including speed limits and seat belt use  
- Regulations around certain medications and substances

Check official government sources or your employer’s guidance on legal matters. Ignorance is not considered an excuse.

### 8.5 Money basics, tipping and gifts

Practical notes:

- The currency is the UAE dirham (AED). Cards are widely accepted, though it is useful to carry some cash.  
- Tipping is appreciated in many service contexts, though not always mandatory. Small amounts for good service in restaurants, taxis and deliveries are common.  
- When giving gifts, choose items that are thoughtful and culturally neutral. Avoid anything that may be inappropriate in a Muslim context, such as alcohol, unless you are absolutely certain it is acceptable in that relationship.

### 8.6 Avoiding burnout and loneliness

Relocation can be tiring. New systems, new climate and new expectations all at once can lead to fatigue.

To support your wellbeing:

- Establish routines for sleep, exercise and meals early  
- Limit how much work you take home, especially in the first months  
- Build small support circles, such as a trusted colleague, a neighbour or a community group  
- Stay in touch with friends and family in the UK, but also invest in local relationships

If you experience persistent low mood, anxiety or a sense of being overwhelmed, seek professional support. Many organisations provide access to counselling or employee assistance programmes.

# Chapter 9 – First 90 Days Plan & Do & Do not Lists

### 9.1 First 30 days

Your first month is about orientation and observation.

Focus on:

- Learning names, roles and key processes  
- Understanding basic workplace etiquette, such as greetings, meeting patterns and communication channels  
- Noticing how colleagues interact with each other and with senior figures  
- Exploring your neighbourhood and essential services such as supermarkets, pharmacies and clinics

Keep a low ego and a high level of curiosity. Aim to ask more questions than you answer.

### 9.2 Days 31 to 60

In your second month, you can start contributing more actively.

Focus on:

- Taking on small projects where you can demonstrate reliability  
- Offering ideas, but framing them as suggestions rather than fixed opinions  
- Continuing to build relationships across departments  
- Revisiting sections of this guide with specific experiences in mind

Ask a trusted colleague for feedback on how your style is coming across. Listen carefully and adjust.

### 9.3 Days 61 to 90

By the third month, you are past the very early stage, but still learning.

Focus on:

- Consolidating routines at work and at home  
- Taking more ownership where you are confident, while staying open to guidance  
- Identifying areas where deeper cultural understanding is needed and seeking opportunities to learn, such as local training or mentoring

You may also experience a second wave of culture shock, where initial excitement gives way to frustration. This is normal. Use the reflection tools in the appendices to process your experience.

### 9.4 Workplace Do List

- Do greet colleagues politely every day  
- Do arrive on time and come prepared for meetings  
- Do adapt your communication style to protect others’ dignity  
- Do show respect for local customs, religious practices and national symbols  
- Do ask questions when you are unsure about cultural expectations  
- Do give credit to others and avoid taking all the spotlight

### 9.5 Workplace Do not List

- Do not assume UK ways of working are always best  
- Do not criticise the UAE, its people or its leaders  
- Do not give harsh feedback in front of others  
- Do not make jokes about religion, culture or gender  
- Do not post negative comments about your employer or host country online  
- Do not rely only on expatriate friends for your information about the UAE

### 9.6 Mini workplace communication guide

**Polite email phrases**

- “I hope this message finds you well.”  
- “Thank you for your time and guidance.”  
- “Kindly find attached the requested documents.”  
- “I would appreciate your advice on the following point.”

**Soft disagreement**

- “I see your point, and I have a different concern that I would like to share.”  
- “Perhaps we could also consider another option that may address X.”

**Clarifying confusion**

- “Just to ensure I have understood correctly, may I summarise what we agreed?”  
- “To avoid any misunderstanding, could you please confirm whether…”

**Saying “no” safely**

- “At the moment, my capacity is limited, and I want to ensure I can deliver good quality work. Would it be possible to adjust the deadline or reduce the scope?”  
- “I would like to help, but this request may be better handled by [team or person], as they are responsible for that area.”

## A1. UK vs UAE Comparison (outline for table)

You can turn this into a two column table in your design tool.

**Communication**

- UK: Tends to value clarity and moderate directness, with some indirect habits  
- UAE: Often more high context, with greater emphasis on tone, politeness and implying rather than stating directly

**Decision making**

- UK: Decisions often made in meetings, with open discussion  
- UAE: Consultative phases, with final decisions sometimes made later by senior leaders

**Time**

- UK: Strong focus on schedules and punctuality  
- UAE: Mix of punctuality in some sectors and greater flexibility in others

**Feedback**

- UK: More direct, sometimes in group settings  
- UAE: Preferably private, framed carefully to protect dignity

**Relationships**

- UK: Task and relationship often seen as parallel tracks  
- UAE: Relationship building often seen as a foundation for tasks and decisions
